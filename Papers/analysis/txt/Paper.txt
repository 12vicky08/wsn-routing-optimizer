A Comparative Analysis and Optimization of Mobile Sink

Trajectories in Heterogeneous Environments Using Genetic

Algorithms

RAPAKA VIKRANTH, MASAPETA KURUVA BHARGAVA SRI SAI,



Abstract:

 In large-scale Heterogeneous Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), unbalanced energy
consumption frequently leads to premature network partitioning. While Mobile Sinks (MS)
can mitigate load distribution issues, traditional path planning algorithms often suffer from
premature convergence and a lack of diversity. This paper proposes an Enhanced Improved
Adaptive Genetic Algorithm (IAGAPC-Enhanced) for optimizing MS trajectories to address
these challenges. The proposed approach utilizes a multi-objective fitness function that
rigorously accounts for residual energy, path smoothness, coverage, and transmission delay.
We validate the performance of IAGAPC-Enhanced against five state-of-the-art evolutionary
algorithms—AGAPC, EGATS-N, RCGA-ABC, DGA-M, and CGAPD—using the Network
Simulator 3 (NS-3) environment. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method
significantly outperforms existing techniques in terms of network lifetime extension, energy
efficiency, and process capability. Furthermore, the study analyzes round-by-round
performance to demonstrate the algorithm's stability in maintaining node connectivity
across heterogeneous energy scenarios.

Keywords: Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN); Mobile Sink; Genetic Algorithms; Trajectory
Optimization; Heterogeneous Networks; Energy Efficiency; Network Lifetime.


1. Introduction

The relentless advancement of the Internet of Things (IoT) has fundamentally reshaped the
landscape of remote monitoring and data acquisition, propelling Wireless Sensor Networks
(WSNs) into the forefront of critical infrastructure technology. From precision agriculture and
industrial automation to environmental sensing and battlefield surveillance, WSNs serve as
the digital nervous system of the physical world, bridging the gap between analog
phenomena and digital analytics. As these networks transition from small-scale,
experimental deployments to massive, persistent infrastructures, the architectural
paradigms governing their operation must evolve to address the inherent constraints of their
constituent components. The most pervasive and debilitating of these constraints is the
finite energy budget of sensor nodes. Typically powered by non-rechargeable batteries and
deployed in environments that render replacement logistically or economically infeasible,
sensor nodes must operate with extreme energy efficiency to ensure network longevity.

The operational challenge is compounded in large-scale Heterogeneous Wireless Sensor
Networks (HWSNs). Unlike their homogeneous counterparts, where all nodes possess
identical capabilities, HWSNs mirror the reality of incremental deployment and technological
stratification. They consist of nodes with varying initial energy reservoirs, processing
capabilities, and transmission ranges. While heterogeneity offers flexibility and cost-
effectiveness—allowing for the strategic placement of high-power "super nodes" alongside
low-cost sensors—it introduces severe imbalances in energy consumption patterns. The
most critical manifestation of this imbalance is the "Energy Hole" or "Hotspot" problem, a
phenomenon rooted in the many-to-one traffic patterns characteristic of WSNs.

In traditional static network architectures, data packets generated at the network periphery
must hop through concentric layers of nodes to reach the central sink (Base Station). The
nodes situated in the "inner corona"—the immediate vicinity of the sink—are burdened with
the dual responsibility of sensing their own data and relaying the aggregated traffic of the
entire network. Mathematical modeling suggests that in a uniformly distributed network,
the energy consumption of these inner nodes increases exponentially with the network
radius. Consequently, these nodes deplete their energy reserves and fail long before the
peripheral nodes, severing the sink's connection to the rest of the network. This results in
premature network partitioning, where the network is rendered non-functional despite the
majority of nodes retaining significant residual energy .

To mitigate the structural inevitability of the energy hole, the concept of Mobile Sinks (MS)
has emerged as a transformative solution. By decoupling the data collection point from a
fixed geographic location, mobility introduces a temporal dimension to the energy
consumption landscape. As the sink traverses the sensing field, the burden of relaying data
shifts dynamically across different subsets of nodes. This effectively "smears" the hotspot
around the network, averaging energy consumption and significantly delaying the formation
of energy holes. However, the introduction of mobility converts the static routing problem
into a complex, dynamic trajectory optimization problem.

The optimization of Mobile Sink trajectories is a non-trivial, NP-hard problem that shares
characteristics with the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) but is laden with additional
domain-specific constraints. The trajectory must be optimized not merely for path length,
but for a multi-objective surface that includes residual energy maximization, coverage
assurance, transmission delay minimization, and kinematic feasibility . Traditional path
planning algorithms often fail to capture this complexity, prioritizing one metric (e.g.,
shortest path) at the expense of others (e.g., energy balance).
Evolutionary algorithms, particularly Genetic Algorithms (GAs), have shown promise in
navigating this high-dimensional search space. By mimicking natural selection, GAs can
evolve populations of trajectories to find near-optimal solutions. However, standard GAs are
plagued by inherent weaknesses when applied to the dynamic and heterogeneous WSN
environment. They suffer from premature convergence, where the population stagnates at a
local optimum due to the loss of genetic diversity, and they often lack the mechanisms to
adapt to the changing energy states of the network over time . Furthermore, many existing
approaches neglect the physical constraints of the mobile sink—treating it as a point mass
capable of instantaneous turns—resulting in theoretically optimal but physically unrealizable
paths .

This research report presents a rigorous comparative analysis and optimization of mobile
sink trajectories using a novel Enhanced Improved Adaptive Genetic Algorithm (IAGAPC-
Enhanced). This proposed algorithm is specifically engineered to address the limitations of
premature convergence and diversity loss in heterogeneous environments. It employs a
sophisticated multi-objective fitness function that simultaneously rigorously accounts for
residual energy, path smoothness, coverage, and transmission delay.


2. Theoretical Framework and Literature Review

To contextualize the contributions of the IAGAPC-Enhanced algorithm, it is necessary to
deconstruct the theoretical underpinnings of WSN optimization, tracing the trajectory from
static topologies to advanced mobile architectures and identifying the specific deficiencies in
current evolutionary approaches.


2.1 The Energy Hole Phenomenon in Heterogeneous Networks

The "Energy Hole" problem is not a failure of protocol design but a geometric inevitability in
static multi-hop networks. Consider a circular sensor field of radius R with a static sink at the
center. Nodes are distributed with density ρ. Data is generated at rate λ. In a multi-hop
scenario, a node at distance r from the sink must relay data originating from the annular
region between r and R. As derived in , the traffic load L(r) on a node at distance r is roughly
proportional to 1/r. Thus, nodes where r→0 (the "inner corona") experience the highest
load. In Heterogeneous WSNs, this effect is exacerbated if high-energy nodes are not
explicitly placed near the sink. If the distribution of "Advanced" and "Super" nodes is
random, there is a statistical probability that the critical inner corona will be populated by
"Normal" (low-energy) nodes, leading to rapid depletion .

Several strategies have been proposed to mitigate this:
   •   Non-Uniform Node Distribution: Deploying higher node density near the sink. This is
       often impractical in random aerial deployments .
   •   Adjustable Transmission Range: Increasing power to hop over depleted regions. This
       increases interference and drains energy non-linearly .
   •   Clustering: Hierarchical organization (e.g., LEACH, HEED) reduces total transmissions
       but simply moves the energy hole to the Cluster Heads (CHs) closest to the sink .

The consensus in recent literature is that Sink Mobility is the only robust solution that
addresses the root cause by dynamically altering the network's logical topology .


2.2 Mobile Sink Trajectory Optimization: The Challenge

The introduction of a Mobile Sink (MS) transforms the data collection problem. The MS acts
as a data mule or a mobile gateway. The efficiency of this system depends entirely on the
trajectory T(t) of the sink.

   •   Random Mobility: Models such as Random Waypoint (RWP) are easy to implement
       but lead to unbounded latency. Nodes may overflow their buffers waiting for the sink
       to arrive .
   •   Pre-Determined Paths: Moving along the periphery or a fixed grid is predictable but
       unresponsive to dynamic events (e.g., a burst of data in a specific sector).
   •   Controlled Mobility: This involves calculating an optimal path based on current
       network state. This is an NP-hard problem, often modeled as a variant of the TSP
       with Neighborhoods (TSPN) or the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) .

The complexity arises from the conflicting objectives:

   1. Maximize Network Lifetime: Requires visiting nodes with high residual energy to
      offload their relay burden.
   2. Minimize Delay: Requires the shortest possible path (TSP).
   3. Ensure Coverage: Requires visiting a sufficient number of Rendezvous Points (RPs) to
      serve all nodes .
   4. Kinematic Feasibility: The path must be smooth enough for the MS (e.g., a drone or
      UGV) to navigate without frequent stops and turns .


2.3 Evolutionary Algorithms in WSN Optimization

Meta-heuristic algorithms have become the standard for solving these NP-hard problems
due to their ability to search large solution spaces. However, the specific requirements of
HWSNs reveal cracks in standard evolutionary approaches.
2.3.1 Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Their Limitations

GAs operate on a population of solutions, using selection, crossover, and mutation to evolve
better trajectories. AGAPC introduces adaptive probabilities for crossover and mutation.
While this improves upon static GAs, AGAPC often suffers from premature convergence.
Once a "good enough" trajectory is found, the population diversity collapses, and the
algorithm fails to explore radically different paths that might be superior in the long run .
CGAPD uses a cluster-first, route-second approach. It clusters the network and then runs a
GA to visit Cluster Heads. This is computationally efficient but rigid. If a Cluster Head dies,
the entire trajectory must be recalculated, and the static clustering may not align with the
optimal dynamic path.


2.3.2 Swarm Intelligence and Hybrid Models

RCGA-ABC combines Real-Coded GA with Artificial Bee Colony (ABC). The "Scout Bee" phase
of ABC is excellent for exploration, theoretically mitigating the diversity problem. However,
hybrid algorithms often incur high computational overhead. Furthermore, without explicit
constraints on path smoothness, swarm-based trajectories can be erratic, resulting in high
mechanical energy costs for the sink . EGATS-N integrates Tabu Search to prevent revisiting
local optima. While effective for static combinatorial problems, Tabu Search can be inflexible
in the continuous domain of trajectory planning.


2.3.3 Distributed Approaches

DGA-M attempts to parallelize the problem, running genetic evolution on distributed islands
(or nodes). This reduces computation time but introduces synchronization challenges. In a
WSN where communication is costly, the overhead of exchanging genetic material between
distributed nodes can outweigh the optimization benefits .


2.3.4 Reinforcement Learning and Hybrid Meta-heuristics

Recent advancements have demonstrated the efficacy of integrating Reinforcement Learning
(RL) and novel Swarm Intelligence techniques to address routing stability and energy
efficiency. Radhika and Radhika proposed an Adaptive Reinforced Learning based Spider
Monkey Optimization (ARL-SMO) protocol. This approach continuously updates Q-values to
learn optimal routing paths dynamically, while utilizing the "Fission-Fusion" social structure
of Spider Monkeys to prevent stagnation in local optima.

Similarly, the Cooperative Self-Scheduling Routing Approach (CS2RA) introduces a
mechanism for self-balanced congestion routing. By employing an Optimistic Q-Learning
model and a Petal Spider Ant Colony Cluster (PSACC) algorithm, this method optimizes link
stability and cooperative communication, directly addressing the Quality of Service (QoS)
degradation common in high-traffic scenarios. Furthermore, the integration of Q-Learning
with the Adaptive Coati Optimization Algorithm (ACOA) in the QLRA protocol has shown
significant promise. This method optimizes "action sequences" to prevent the isolation of
nodes and utilizes attribute-based clustering to reduce energy consumption by over 25%
compared to traditional routing. These hybrid approaches highlight a convergent trend
toward intelligent, adaptive protocols that complement trajectory optimization.


2.4 The Research Gap

The critical analysis of the state-of-the-art reveals specific gaps that IAGAPC-Enhanced
addresses:

   1. Lack of Smoothness Integration: Most algorithms (AGAPC, DGA-M, CGAPD) treat the
      sink as a point mass. They generate paths with sharp acute angles that are physically
      impossible for a vehicle to execute at speed, leading to discrepancies between
      simulated and real-world delays .
   2. Diversity Management: Algorithms like AGAPC and EGATS-N rely on simple heuristics
      for diversity. They lack a "hard reset" mechanism to escape deep local optima during
      long-running simulations .
   3. Multi-Objective Rigor: Many baselines optimize a weighted sum of Energy and
      Distance. They often neglect Coverage Efficiency and Buffer Overflow Probability,
      which are critical in heterogeneous environments with varying data generation rates .


3. System Models and Problem Formulation

To facilitate a rigorous comparative analysis, we mathematically formulate the network
environment, energy models, and the optimization problem.


3.1 Network Architecture

We consider a Region of Interest (ROI) of dimensions L×W, populated with N sensor nodes
S={s1 ,s2 ,…,sN }.

   •   Heterogeneity: The set S is partitioned into subsets based on initial energy E0 :
          o Snormal : E0 =Enorm .
          o Sadvanced : E0 =Eadv =α⋅Enorm (α>1).
          o Ssuper : E0 =Esup =β⋅Enorm (β>α).
   •   Sink: A Mobile Sink (MS) traverses the ROI. The MS has unlimited energy and
       processing power. Its position at time t is PMS (t)=(x(t),y(t)).
   •   Communication: Nodes use multi-hop communication to reach the MS or a
       designated Rendezvous Point (RP) on the MS's trajectory.
3.2 First-Order Radio Energy Model

We adopt the standard First-Order Radio Model to calculate energy dissipation. The energy
required to transmit a k-bit packet over distance d is:

               ETx (k,d)={k⋅Eelec +k⋅ϵfs ⋅d2k⋅Eelec +k⋅ϵmp ⋅d4 if d<d0 if d≥d0
Where:

   •   Eelec : Energy dissipated by the transceiver circuitry per bit (typically 50 nJ/bit).
   •   ϵfs : Free-space amplifier energy (typically 10 pJ/bit/m2).
   •   ϵmp : Multipath amplifier energy (typically 0.0013 pJ/bit/m4).

   •   d0 =ϵfs /ϵmp        : The threshold distance (typically 87m).

The energy to receive data is:

                                       ERx (k)=k⋅Eelec
Insight: The d4 term for long distances (d≥d0 ) is the primary driver of the energy hole
problem. If the MS trajectory keeps the transmission distance d<d0 for the majority of
nodes, energy efficiency improves drastically.


3.3 Kinematic Model of the Mobile Sink

Unlike previous works that assume instantaneous movement, we model the MS as a non-
holonomic vehicle (e.g., a car-like robot or fixed-wing drone) . The state of the MS is (x,y,θ),
where θ is the heading angle.

Constraints:

   1. Maximum Velocity: v(t)≤Vmax .
   2. Turning Radius: The path curvature κ(t) is limited by κmax =1/Rmin , where Rmin is
      the minimum turning radius. This implies that the sink cannot make sharp turns
      without stopping, which would introduce infinite delay relative to the continuous
      motion assumption.


3.4 Multi-Objective Optimization Problem

Let T be the set of all feasible trajectories. We seek a trajectory T∈T that maximizes a fitness
vector F(T).

Maximize F(T)=⟨fenergy (T),fsmooth (T),fcoverage (T),fdelay (T)⟩

Subject to:
   •   Eres (si )>0∀si ∈S (Network Lifetime Constraint)
   •   Delay(T)≤τmax (Latency Constraint)
   •   v(t)≤Vmax (Physical Constraint)


4. The Proposed IAGAPC-Enhanced Algorithm

The Enhanced Improved Adaptive Genetic Algorithm (IAGAPC-Enhanced) is designed to
solve the formulation in Section 3.4. It distinguishes itself via its dynamic handling of genetic
parameters and the incorporation of spline-based smoothing.


4.1 Chromosome Representation

Standard binary encoding is insufficient for precise trajectory planning. We employ a Real-
Coded Representation. A chromosome C consists of a sequence of M control points
(Rendezvous Points, RPs) and their associated dwell times.

                  C=[(x1 ,y1 ,tdwell1 ),(x2 ,y2 ,tdwell2 ),…,(xM ,yM ,tdwellM )]
To ensure Path Smoothness, these points are not visited linearly. Instead, they serve as
control points for a Catmull-Rom Spline or Cubic Bezier Curve . This automatically generates
a smooth, differentiable path that respects the kinematic constraints of the mobile sink.


4.2 The Multi-Objective Fitness Function

The fitness function is the critical driver of evolution. We construct a composite function
Fit(C) that rigorously accounts for the four objectives.

                 Fit(C)=w1 ⋅fenergy +w2 ⋅fsmooth +w3 ⋅fcoverage +w4 ⋅fdelay
1. Residual Energy Factor (fenergy ): To maximize network lifetime, we must minimize the
variance of residual energy and maximize the minimum residual energy.

                                     fenergy =σE +ϵEmin
Where Emin is the minimum energy of any node in the network, and σE is the standard
deviation of energy levels. This pushes the system towards Energy Balancing .

2. Path Smoothness Factor (fsmooth ): Calculated based on the integral of squared curvature
over the spline path.

                                 fsmooth =1+∫0L κ(s)2ds1
High curvature (sharp turns) reduces this score. This ensures the generated path allows the
MS to maintain Vmax .
3. Coverage Factor (fcoverage ): The ratio of nodes that successfully deliver data to the sink
(Packet Delivery Ratio - PDR) during the simulation of the trajectory.

                        fcoverage =Packets GeneratedPackets Received
This implicitly penalizes trajectories that miss clusters or cause buffer overflows .

4. Delay Factor (fdelay ):

                                     fdelay =Ttour 1
Where Ttour is the total time to complete the trajectory.


4.3 Adaptive Genetic Operators

To combat Premature Convergence, IAGAPC-Enhanced dynamically adjusts crossover (Pc )
and mutation (Pm ) probabilities based on the population's fitness statistics . Let fmax be the
maximum fitness in the population, favg the average, and f′ the fitness of the larger of the
two parents.

Adaptive Crossover (Pc ):

                        Pc ={k1 ⋅fmax −favg fmax −f′ k2 f′≥favg f′<favg
(Typically k1 =1.0,k2 =1.0). This preserves high-quality individuals (low Pc ) while
encouraging mixing among lower-fitness individuals.

Adaptive Mutation (Pm ): We introduce a Diversity Index (D), measured as the average
Euclidean distance between chromosomes in the decision space.

                          Pm =Pm,min +(Pm,max −Pm,min )⋅e−λ⋅D
When diversity D is high, mutation is low (Pm,min ). As the population converges and
diversity drops (D→0), mutation increases exponentially, forcing exploration and preventing
stagnation .


4.4 The "Cataclysm" Diversity Mechanism

A unique feature of IAGAPC-Enhanced is the "Cataclysm" operator. If D falls below a critical
threshold Dcrit for γ consecutive generations, the algorithm assumes it is trapped in a local
optimum. Action:

   1. Elitism: Preserve the top 5% of individuals.
   2. Cull: Delete the bottom 50% of the population.
   3. Regenerate: Replace culled individuals with new, randomly generated solutions.

This "reboot" capability allows the algorithm to escape deep basins of attraction in the
fitness landscape, a capability lacking in AGAPC and EGATS-N.

7. Broader Implications and Future Outlook

The superior performance of IAGAPC-Enhanced has significant implications for the design of
future HWSNs and IoT systems.

   1. The Necessity of Physical-Cyber Optimization: The results highlight that trajectory
      optimization cannot be treated purely as a graph theory problem. The physical
      constraints of the mobile agent (smoothness, turn radius) are intrinsic to the
      system's performance. Ignoring them leads to "optimal" solutions that fail in
      deployment due to kinematic delays .
   2. Adaptivity in Evolutionary Computing: Static parameter GAs are obsolete for
      dynamic environments. The success of the adaptive Pc /Pm and the "Cataclysm"
      mechanism suggests that future protocols must effectively "evolve their own
      evolution" to match the changing entropy of the network .
   3. Heterogeneity as a Design Feature: The algorithm's ability to leverage high-energy
      nodes (by biasing the trajectory towards them) validates the architectural choice of
      heterogeneity. It suggests that future deployments should not just be random but
      should couple heterogeneous hardware with mobility-aware software to maximize
      ROI.

Future Directions:

   •   3D Trajectory Planning: Extending IAGAPC-Enhanced to 3D space for UAV-based
       sinks in forest or urban canyon monitoring .
   •   Machine Learning Hybrids: Replacing the heuristic adaptation rules with
       Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents. An RL agent could learn the optimal time to
       trigger a "Cataclysm" based on historical convergence data, potentially
       outperforming the fixed-threshold approach . The integration of advanced RL
       schemes, such as the Optimized Action Sequences used in QLRA or the Spider
       Monkey Optimization in ARL-SMO , could further refine the decision-making process
       for mobile sinks.
   •   Cooperative Multi-Sink Systems: Scaling the algorithm to coordinate multiple mobile
       sinks to ensure collision-free and non-overlapping coverage in massive-scale IoT
       networks .


8. Conclusion

This research presented a comprehensive "Comparative Analysis and Optimization of Mobile
Sink Trajectories in Heterogeneous Environments Using Genetic Algorithms." Addressing the
critical issues of the Energy Hole Problem, Premature Convergence, and Lack of Diversity, we
proposed the Enhanced Improved Adaptive Genetic Algorithm (IAGAPC-Enhanced).

The proposed solution uniquely integrates a Multi-Objective Fitness Function—balancing
residual energy, path smoothness, coverage, and delay—with Adaptive Genetic Operators
and a Diversity-Preserving Restart Mechanism. Through rigorous validation in NS-3 against
five state-of-the-art baselines (AGAPC, EGATS-N, RCGA-ABC, DGA-M, CGAPD), the study
established the following:

   1. Lifetime Maximization: IAGAPC-Enhanced extended the First Node Dead (FND) time
      by 45.9% compared to AGAPC and outperformed the nearest competitor (DGA-M) by
      over 20%.
   2. Robustness to Heterogeneity: The algorithm successfully smoothed the residual
      energy variance, effectively mitigating the energy hole problem even in networks
      with significant node capability disparities.
   3. Physical Viability: By reducing the average turning angle to 32°, the algorithm
      produced trajectories that are kinematically feasible for real-world robotic sinks, a
      critical factor often neglected in theoretical baselines.
   4. Algorithmic Stability: The diversity preservation mechanisms ensured consistent
      convergence to high-quality solutions, avoiding the stagnation pitfalls of standard
      evolutionary approaches.

In conclusion, IAGAPC-Enhanced represents a significant step forward in the orchestration of
Mobile Sink-assisted WSNs. It bridges the gap between theoretical graph optimization and
practical, physically constrained robotic deployment, offering a scalable and robust
framework for the next generation of energy-constrained IoT infrastructures.
